Ask a Houston homeowner about their soil and you’ll often hear one word: gumbo. Much of the region sits on expansive clay that turns sticky and heavy when wet and cracks into hard plates in a dry summer. It’s a big part of why yards here drain so slowly, and why water around a foundation deserves attention.

This guide explains how clay behaves, why drainage matters for the ground under your slab, and where a drainage company’s work ends and a foundation professional’s begins.

## What makes Houston clay different

Expansive clay is made of very fine particles that absorb water and swell, then release it and shrink. The changes can be significant: in a long dry spell, you’ll see wide cracks open in the lawn and gaps where the soil pulls away from the edge of the slab. After weeks of rain, the same soil is saturated and swollen.

Clay also drains slowly. Water that lands on it tends to sit on top or move sideways rather than soaking in. Combine that with Houston’s flat ground and heavy rain, and you get yards that hold water for days.

## Why moisture swings near the slab matter

Most Houston homes sit on slab-on-grade foundations that rest directly on this soil. When the clay under and around a slab swells and shrinks unevenly — wetter on one side, drier on another — the ground supporting the foundation moves unevenly too.

Uneven moisture usually comes from things around the house:

- **Downspouts** dumping hundreds of gallons at one corner in every storm.
- **Beds and mulch** built up against the slab that trap water and send it toward the house.
- **Low spots** next to the foundation where water collects and sits.
- **Patios and walks** that settled and now pitch toward the house.
- **Large trees** pulling moisture from one side of the house during dry months.

Good drainage aims for *consistent* moisture: water from storms moves away from the foundation instead of soaking one area, so the soil around the house isn’t swinging from one extreme to the other.

## Signs water is collecting at your foundation

- Puddles along the slab that remain after a storm passes.
- Soil washed away from the edge of the slab, exposing more concrete than before.
- Mulch and soil splashed onto walls and windows.
- Flowerbeds along the house that stay soggy or smell sour.
- Water marks on the garage floor or along interior baseboards near exterior walls.

## Drainage habits that help

**Slope the ground away from the house.** Soil and beds next to the foundation should fall away from it, so water heads out into the yard rather than back toward the slab. Over the years, mulch and new plantings tend to build up and reverse that slope.

**Get roof water away from the slab.** Downspouts carry most of the water your property handles in a storm. Extensions help; buried solid pipe to a curb outlet or pop-up emitter is better. Our [downspout drainage guide](/resources/downspout-drainage-guide/) covers the options.

**Give low spots an outlet.** If water collects in a corner by the house, a catch basin or French drain can collect it and carry it away. If the corner sits lower than the street, a pump may be needed.

**Keep drains working.** Silt, leaves and roots slowly fill catch basins and lines. A drain that’s half full only does half the job.

**Avoid creating new problems.** Don’t bury downspouts into the yard with no outlet, don’t pile fresh beds against the slab, and don’t aim sprinklers at the foundation.
