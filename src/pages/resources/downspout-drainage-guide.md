Gutters get the attention, but downspouts decide where your roof water ends up. In Houston, with heavy clay and flat yards, a downspout that dumps at the corner of the house can do more damage than almost anything else on the lot. This guide covers the math, the options and the mistakes to avoid.

## The math: how much water your roof sheds

Every inch of rain on every square foot of roof produces about **0.623 gallons** of water. That number adds up fast.

Take a common example: a home with a **2,400-square-foot roof** footprint in a **3-inch** rain:

- 2,400 sq ft × 3 in × 0.623 = about **4,486 gallons**
- With **4 downspouts**, that’s roughly **1,121 gallons per downspout**

That’s more than twenty 55-gallon drums landing at each corner of the house in a single storm. A tropical system can deliver several times that.

## Your options, from simplest to best

| Option | How it works | Good for | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Splash block | A small tray under the downspout | Light rain on well-sloped ground | Water still lands within a couple of feet of the slab |
| Extension | A section of pipe or flexible spout aimed out into the yard | Quick, cheap improvement | Easy to knock loose, mow over or trip on; still dumps in the yard |
| Buried solid pipe to a pop-up emitter | Underground pipe carries water to a cap that pops open and releases it in the lawn | Yards with a lower area away from the house | The release point has to be lower and far enough away to drain |
| Buried solid pipe to a curb outlet | Underground pipe carries water to the street | Most Houston lots with curb and gutter | Needs enough fall to the curb; outlet must follow local rules |

For most Houston homes, buried solid pipe is the long-term answer. It takes roof water away from the slab, keeps the yard usable and doesn’t depend on anyone remembering to put an extension back.

## How a good downspout system is built

- **Solid pipe, not perforated.** Roof water should travel in a sealed pipe to its outlet. Perforated pipe belongs in French drains, where you want water to enter the pipe — not leak out of it next to the foundation.
- **Consistent slope.** The pipe needs to fall steadily toward the outlet so water and debris keep moving.
- **A real outlet.** Curb outlet, pop-up emitter, or a tie-in to a properly designed drainage system. Never a dead end in the yard.
- **Clean-outs.** Access points let you clear the line without digging it up.
- **Leaf protection.** Gutter guards or strainers at the top cut down on debris entering the line.
- **Catch basins where needed.** A basin at the base of a downspout, or in a low corner nearby, collects water and debris before it enters the pipe.

## Common mistakes

**Burying the downspout into the yard with no outlet.** It looks tidy, but the water just fills the pipe and soaks into the soil right next to the house, which is exactly what you were trying to avoid.

**Using perforated pipe for roof water.** Perforated pipe lets water out along its length. Near the foundation, that defeats the purpose.

**Pipes that are too small or too flat.** Undersized or nearly level pipe backs up in heavy rain and fills with silt.

**Discharging onto a neighbor’s property.** Redirecting water onto the lot next door creates a new problem and, often, a dispute.

**Forgetting maintenance.** Leaves and roof grit slowly fill lines. Check pop-up emitters and clean-outs before hurricane season.

## Signs your downspouts need attention

- Puddles or washed-out soil at the base of downspouts.
- Mulch and dirt splashed onto walls.
- Gutters overflowing even when they’re clean (often a clogged downspout or buried line).
- Pop-up emitters that never pop up during rain.
- Water standing in flowerbeds next to the house.
